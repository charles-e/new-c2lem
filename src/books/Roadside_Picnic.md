<blockquote>
Suddenly, for no apparent reason, he felt a wave of despair. Everything was useless. Everything was pointless. My God, he thought, we can’t do a thing! We can’t stop it, we can’t slow it down! No force in the world could contain this blight, he thought in horror. It’s not because we do bad work. And it’s not because they are more clever and cunning than we are. The world is just like that. Man is like that. If it wasn’t the Visit, it would have been something else. Pigs can always find mud.
</blockquote>

The story, mainly, follows Redrick "Red" Schuhardt, a "stalker", an illegal scavenger of the "Zone", that is: a place where aliens have visited earth and left artifacts of advanced technology.  However visiting a zone is extremely hazardous because aliens have also left inexplicable traps, for example, early in the story, during a zone expedition, Redrick's partner, loses both legs to a trap that is never seen. Additionally the zone visits have long term subtle effects on the stalkers, such as Red's toddler daughter whose nickname is "Monkey" for her apparent hairyness: this is described very matter-of-factly.  
The story continues over 10 years (a guess b/c Red is caught and goes to prison so his daughter is at least a teenager during her later appearance) and stalkers are replaced largely by robots. But mankind has accepted that they have intelligent company in the universe, even if the aliens are only evident through artifacts.  
The story final part is Redrick's allegedly final zone visit, his latest partner, the son of the partner who lost his legs, dies during the visit.  The story ends with Red approaching the target artifact that seems to have killed his partner, with Red seeming to pray to the aliens and accept his seeming animal-hood relative to the aliens.  
This book is notable for the being the first(?) story describing what it might be like for humanity to live as a "cargo cult" to alien artifacts and garbage.  So props for that!  However this book has a large amount of very tedious verbiage, which in describing what it would be like to visit a real "zone" which is laced with nearly undetectable traps, is completely justifiable.  However this book, in general, is not one for beautiful prose, more for sarcasm which is reminiscent of Heinlein.
