<blockquote>
You don’t remember why you are there, don’t remember traveling to this house. You do not perseverate on such thoughts, though, which are distant, nagging annoyances, and not as pressing a matter as the in-media-res conversation and your hummingbird-wings eyelids. You mash your palms into your eyes again. Your hands sink inside your head, to the depth of your wrists, mercifully stopping the manic, strobing flutter. Still, somehow, you are able to see. You are marooned on a pea-green couch, a long one that extends to the edges of your periphery. You sit slouched and your legs are vines growing haphazardly into the room.
</blockquote>

This book started as a great idea and it has some really nice prose, but I did not love the story because it moved too slowly for me. Basically we are following two points of view, Julia a twenty-two year old unmotivated college grad whose day job is as a waitress and is pursuing a tv(?) career. But mom calls her up for an interview at a thinly veiled google-like tech company where thanks to video game experience and the fact that mom is actually a VP at the company, she is hired to pilot a cybernetically enhanced zombie to across the country on two airline flights.  The other point of view that of the zombie, called Bernie for much of the story, whose consciousness is actually being rebuilt by some other entity. 

I enjoyed the chapters narrated by Julia, who has a bad habit of parroting dialog from [The Big Lebowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Big_Lebowski), and is in general a pleasantly sarcastic young woman.  There are also interludes where the author speaks directly to the reader and these are very amusing.  But the chapters from Bernie's POV are difficult because they are meant to be a simplified fantasy overlay on reality and I just found those chapters too abstracted
